# The 3-minute pitch kit 🎤

I've mentored or judged at nine hackathons. Here's what I see every time: the team with the best code doesn't always win. The team that makes the judges' job easy does.

Judges have a score sheet, a timer and a long queue of teams. Everything below is about winning those few minutes.

## 3 hacks that work on judges

1. **Use the exact words from the judging criteria.** Find the criteria on the event page or ask an organiser. If it says "impact", say "impact" and show it. You're filling in the score sheet for the judge.
2. **Open with one person's problem, not your tech stack.** "This is Meena. Every week she…" Judges forget frameworks by the next team. They remember a person.
3. **Make the sponsor's tool the core, not a sticker.** A sponsor's judge is asking one question: "Is our tool the core, or a sticker?" If you can remove their tool and your app still works, it's a sticker.

## The 3-minute pitch

| Time | What you say |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | **The problem as one person's story.** One person, one pain, in plain words. No statistics yet. |
| 0:30 | **Live demo, one flow.** Show that person's problem getting solved, start to finish. One flow, not a feature tour. |
| 2:00 | **Why it matters.** Who uses it, how many people, what changes for them. Use numbers you can name a source for. |
| 2:30 | **What's next.** What you'd build next, and who on the team built what (one line each). |
| 3:00 | **Stop.** Even mid-sentence. Going over time looks unprepared. |

Got 2 minutes or 5? Keep the same order and the same shares: about a sixth on the problem, half on the demo, the rest on impact and next steps.

### Fill in the blanks

> This is **[one person]**. Every **[day / week]**, they **[the painful thing]**, and it costs them **[time / money / risk]**.
>
> We built **[name]**. Let me show you. *(live demo: one flow)*
>
> **[Number]** people have this problem. With **[name]**, **[what changes]**.
>
> Next, we'd **[one concrete next step]**. I'm **[name]**, I built **[part]**; **[teammate]** built **[part]**. Thank you.

## Match the judging criteria

Read the criteria before you write any code, and again before you write the pitch. These are the words you'll see most often, and what a judge wants to hear for each.

| If the criteria say… | Show them… |
|---|---|
| Innovation / creativity / originality | One line on what exists today and why yours is different. |
| Technical complexity / implementation | The hardest thing you built during the event, named out loud. |
| Impact / usefulness | Who uses it and what changes for them, with a number. |
| Design / user experience | The demo itself. Clean screens, no dev console, no lorem ipsum. |
| Completeness / functionality | A live flow that works end to end. Half-built features stay hidden. |
| Presentation | Time it. Finish under the limit. |

## Questions judges actually ask

Assign each question to one teammate. Answer in 2–3 sentences.

1. Who will actually use this? Have you spoken to one of them?
2. What did you build during the hackathon, and what did you bring ready-made?
3. Why not just use ChatGPT for this?
4. What already exists? How is yours better?
5. Where does your data come from? Is it real or dummy?
6. How accurate is it? How did you test that?
7. What happens when the model or the API is wrong or down?
8. What does it cost to run, per user per month?
9. How does it handle 10,000 users? A million?
10. How do you protect users' personal data?
11. What was the hardest part to build?
12. What would you do in the next 3 months with support?

"We don't know yet, here's how we'd find out" beats bluffing. Every single time.

### What sponsor judges ask

1. Why did you need our tool? What breaks if you take it out?
2. Which of our features did you use, and why those?
3. What was hard about using it? (They want feedback. Be honest.)

## Sponsor-prize checklist

Sponsor prizes are judged separately from the main prizes, often by the sponsor's own team.

**Before the event**

- [ ] Read every sponsor's prize page. Note exactly what each one asks for.
- [ ] Pick 2–3 sponsors whose requirements overlap with your idea.
- [ ] Check the rules: can one project enter more than one sponsor prize? It usually can, but the limit changes by event.

**During the build**

- [ ] Make their tool the core. Removal test: if you take it out, your app should break.
- [ ] Talk to the sponsor's team on day 1. Show a rough version and ask what would impress them.
- [ ] Use their docs and their recommended setup, not a workaround.

**At submission**

- [ ] README with a "How we used [sponsor]" section for each sponsor.
- [ ] A 2-minute demo video and a deployed link.
- [ ] Tick the right prize tracks on the submission form.

**In the pitch**

- [ ] Say the sponsor's tool by name.
- [ ] Show it working in the demo, not only on a slide.
- [ ] One line on why you couldn't have built it without them.

## Presentation checklist

**Slides (5 is enough)**

1. The person and their problem.
2. Live demo (one title slide, then switch to the app).
3. How it works: one diagram, sponsor tools labelled.
4. Impact: one or two numbers, with sources.
5. Team, next steps, and a QR code to the repo or the live link.

**Slide rules**

- One idea per slide, readable from the back of the room.
- No code screenshots. Judges won't read them.
- Put screenshots of the key screens on the last slides, in case the demo fails.

**On the day**

- [ ] Rehearse 5 times with a timer.
- [ ] The person demoing is not the person talking.
- [ ] Record a 60–90 second backup video of the demo flow. Keep it on a laptop and a phone.
- [ ] Carry a phone hotspot. Venue Wi-Fi often dies when every team demos at once.
- [ ] **The 10-second rule:** if the live demo stalls for 10 seconds, calmly switch to the video. Never debug in front of the judges.
